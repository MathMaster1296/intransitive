          Tip

          How to play

          The whole rulebook fits on this page, and a first game takes about ten minutes. If you would rather learn on the board, the walks you through it.

          The board

          The board is nine squares by nine. Blue's home corner is a1 at the bottom left and red's is i9 at the top right. Each side starts with ten pieces: three rocks, four papers and three scissors, arranged in three diagonal rows with the scissors closest to the middle. Blue moves first.

          Moving

          On your turn you must move exactly one of your pieces one square in any direction: sideways, up and down, or diagonally. Every piece moves the same way. Passing is not allowed.

          Capturing

          Rock captures scissors, scissors captures paper, and paper captures rock. To capture, move your piece onto an enemy piece it beats, and the enemy piece is removed. You can never move onto one of your own pieces, and you can never move onto an enemy piece your piece does not beat, which includes an enemy piece of the same type. You cannot move there at all.

          There are no ties and no bounces. A rock next to a rock just sits there.

          Winning

          The first player to move any piece into the opponent's home corner wins. Blue is aiming for i9 and red is aiming for a1. It does not matter which piece gets there, and capturing into the corner counts.

          Because a piece can only be captured by the type that beats it, a piece sitting in its own corner is a wall against two of the three enemy types. That is the basis of passive defence, which comes up a lot in the strategy notes.

          Two rules you will rarely need

          There is no stalemate. If it is your turn and you have no legal move, you lose immediately. In practice this almost only happens when you have no pieces left.

          There is a stagnation rule. If 100 moves go by with no capture at all, the game is a draw. There is no draw by repetition. Most games are decided well before move 50, so this rule mostly matters against very defensive opponents.

          Notation

          Moves are written as the starting square, a dash or an x for a capture, and the destination, so c5-d6 is a quiet move and d6xd7 is a capture. Moves are numbered in pairs the way chess does it. The move list on the play page uses this format, and the Moves button lets you copy a game out or paste one in.

          Chapter 1

          The cheat sheet

          Six habits that decide most games. Each one gets a chapter of its own below, with positions you can play through.

          Count pieces by type

          Count each type separately, because the total hides the story. One scissors against three papers is losing.

          Count rings

          Every runner is a countdown. Your blocker has to stay a ring ahead of it.

          Block with the right type

          A corner blocker must be a piece the runner cannot capture: the same type, or the type that beats it.

          Capture when you can

          Taking the runner ends the threat. Blocking only postpones it.

          Scissors first, papers behind

          A paper walking forward on its own is food for enemy scissors.

          Two moves is a lot

          A trapped piece can wait. A race for the corner cannot.

          Next chapter: the three advantages
          Chapter 2

          The three advantages

          Every position can be read through three questions. Who has more pieces? Who is closer to the enemy corner? And who has the better mix of types? A player who holds two of the three usually wins.

          Material is the simple one: you have more pieces than the other side. It is exactly as good as it sounds, with one twist covered under matchup.

          Position comes in two sizes. Macro position means your pieces are closer to the enemy corner than theirs are to yours. A cramped opponent has fewer useful moves, and more importantly the endgame is a race, so trading down into an endgame favours whoever is already further forward. Micro position is about where pieces stand relative to each other: an isolated enemy scissors with your rock nearby is a target, and a piece attacking two things at once is a fork.

          Matchup is the one that makes this game different. Each of your piece types is only worth something if the enemy has pieces it can capture, and each enemy type is only dangerous if you have pieces it can capture. A side with a single scissors against several enemy papers is in trouble, because one scissors can only answer one paper at a time. Two papers attacking two different places will break through.

          Chapter 3

          Rings: the only distance that matters

          Pieces move like kings, so the number of moves between a piece and a corner is the larger of its distance along the files and its distance along the ranks. Draw every square that is one move from a corner, then two, then three, and you get a set of L-shaped bands. Those are the rings.

          Rings turn a race into arithmetic. A runner on ring 4 needs four moves. A blocker on ring 3 needs three. Who moves first settles the rest. You will count rings before almost every move in the middle and end of a game, and the Rings button on the play page paints them on the board so you do not have to count on your fingers.

          Next chapter: stopping a runner
          Chapter 4

          Stopping a runner

          Sooner or later an enemy piece slips past your pieces and heads for your corner. You have two ways to stop it. A passive defence puts a piece the runner cannot capture into the corner, so the runner arrives and finds the door locked. An active defence captures the runner with the type that beats it. Both depend on rings.

          R

          The ring rule. If it is your move, your blocker must be on the runner's ring or closer to the corner. If it is the runner's move, your blocker must already be a ring closer. Then step inward every time the runner does, and you arrive first.

          Which pieces can block?

          A blocker sitting in the corner only works if the runner cannot capture it. That means the same type as the runner, or the type that beats it. The third type just gets captured, corner and all, which is a loss.

          The same-side exception

          Active defenders get a little slack. A piece that beats the runner can catch it from one ring behind, as long as both are on the same side of the ring, because it can cut across into the runner's path and shadow it along the edge. From the other side of the ring it never catches up, and two rings behind is always too far.

          S

          Same side, one ring behind: catchable. Other side or two rings behind: block the corner instead, or lose.

          More than one runner

          A blocker stops the two types it does not lose to. Two runners of different types therefore need a blocker that beats neither of them, and there is exactly one such type for each pair. If you do not have it, you need to capture one runner and block the other, and the ring rule applies to both jobs at once.

          Position lab

          Set up any race and let the engine settle it. Place a runner and a defender, say who moves, and ask. The engine searches the position to the end and reports a forced win or a hold, with the best first move drawn on the board. Try the presets first, then move the pieces one square at a time and watch the verdict flip.

          Next chapter: trades
          Chapter 6

          Trades

          Each side starts with four papers but only three rocks and three scissors, so losing a paper costs less than losing a rock or a scissors. A trade that leaves you with three of each and your opponent with a hole in their distribution is a good trade even at equal material. A trade that leaves you with one scissors is usually a bad one, unless you get real position out of it.

          The test is always the same. Before you accept or offer a trade, picture the piece counts afterwards for both sides. If the picture is balanced for you and lopsided for them, take it. If it is the other way round, decline, even when the capture looks free.

          The extreme case is a sacrifice for matchup. If the enemy loses their last scissors, every one of your papers becomes immortal, and a lone immortal paper can beat three pieces in the endgame. Sometimes giving up a piece to remove that last scissors is the best move on the board.

          Next chapter: tactics
          Chapter 7

          Tactics

          Three patterns come up in almost every game. Learn to see them a move before they happen.

          Forks

          A piece attacking two enemy pieces it beats will usually win one of them, because the enemy can only move one piece per turn. Papers fork rocks, scissors fork papers, rocks fork scissors. Loose pieces standing two squares apart are asking for it.

          Traps

          A piece with no safe square is as good as captured. Edges and corners make traps easy, because a piece on the edge has only five neighbours and a piece in the corner has three. Do not rush to collect a trapped piece: it is not going anywhere, and the move is often better spent elsewhere.

          Tempo

          Two moves is a lot. Two moves is often enough to win outright, so when you have the choice between collecting a trapped piece and starting a race, count the race first.

          No stalemate

          A player with no legal move loses on the spot. It is rare with many pieces on the board, but at the very end a lone piece can be boxed in by pieces it cannot capture, and that wins the game immediately.

          Next chapter: openings
          Chapter 8

          Openings

          The opening is the least dramatic part of the game, but a few habits pay off. Both sides start with the scissors in front, the papers behind them and the rocks nearest the corner, and that order is worth keeping. Scissors are the pieces that can meet the enemy's front line safely, because the enemy's scissors cannot capture them. A paper that walks forward on its own runs into those scissors and dies.

          The engine's favourite first moves are all scissors moves, and its least favourite are all paper advances. That is not a coincidence.

          e3-f3Engine's top pick. The right-hand scissors steps toward the centre.
          e3-f4The same scissors, one square further forward.
          d4-e5The middle scissors takes the long diagonal.
          Avoid d3-e4, c4-d5, b5-c6Papers stepping in front of the scissors line. The engine rates these worst.

          After that, develop toward the centre, keep pieces of different types near each other so every enemy attacker has something to fear, and do not trade until the counts afterwards favour you. The first player to leave a piece alone in the middle usually loses it.

          Next chapter: endgames
          Chapter 9

          Endgames

          Once the board thins out, macro position and matchup decide almost everything, and the variety is enormous. One rock, one paper and one scissors against two rocks and a paper plays nothing like two papers against a rock and a scissors. Experienced players say that after dozens of games they have still never seen the same endgame twice.

          Some patterns do repeat. The stronger side usually parks one piece in its own corner as a permanent block and walks the others forward together. An immortal piece, one the enemy has no answer to, wins on its own if it is escorted. And the ring rule decides every race, so the side that counts first is the side that wins.

          The best way to learn endgames is to play them. Each position below starts you against the computer with the side to move.

          Next chapter: before every move
          Chapter 10

          Before every move

          Run this list every turn until it becomes automatic. It takes ten seconds and catches most blunders.

          1. What is attacked? Look at every one of your pieces next to an enemy piece that beats it. Turn on Threats on the play page if you want the board to show you.
          2. Can I capture safely? A free piece is a free piece. Check that nothing recaptures, and check the counts afterwards.
          3. Is anything running? Find the enemy piece closest to your corner and count its ring. Find your best blocker and count its ring. Apply the ring rule.
          4. Am I running? Do the same for your closest piece and their best blocker. If you win the race, everything else can wait.
          5. What do the counts look like after this move? Any trade or sacrifice is judged by the distribution it leaves behind.
          6. Can I make two threats at once? A fork, a runner plus an attack, or two runners of different types. One threat gets answered; two often do not.
          Next: glossary
          Chapter 11

          Glossary

          Ring
          All the squares that are the same number of moves from a corner. Ring 1 is the three squares next to it.
          Runner
          Any piece heading for the enemy corner with a real chance of getting there.
          Passive defence
          Parking a piece the runner cannot capture in your own corner.
          Active defence
          Capturing the runner with the type that beats it.
          Blocker
          The piece doing a passive defence. It must be the runner's own type or the type that beats it.
          Matchup
          How the two sides' mixes of rocks, papers and scissors compare. Lopsided is bad.
          Immortal piece
          A piece the enemy can no longer capture because they have none of the type that beats it.
          Overload
          Giving one defender two jobs at once, for example two papers against one scissors.
          Fork
          One piece attacking two enemy pieces it beats.
          Trap
          A piece with no safe square to move to. As good as captured.
          Tempo
          A move. Wasting one is often the difference between winning and losing a race.
          Stagnation
          The draw that ends a game after 100 moves with no capture.

          About this site

          The site is plain HTML, CSS and JavaScript with no build step, and it runs entirely in your browser. The rules engine follows the game's rules exactly, including the no-stalemate rule and the draw after 100 moves without a capture. It works offline once it has loaded, and you can install it to your home screen.

          The computer opponent is an alpha-beta search that runs in a background thread. Its evaluation is a direct translation of the strategy notes: it counts material, penalises lopsided distributions, rewards pieces that are closer to the enemy corner, and checks whether any runner can still be stopped using the ring rule. Easy looks two moves ahead and makes deliberate mistakes, medium looks four moves ahead, hard searches as deep as it can in about two seconds, and the adaptive level picks a strength to match your rating. The four personalities weight the same evaluation differently. The same search drives the evaluation bar, the hints, the top-moves overlay, the move ratings, the game report and the puzzle checks.

          Your games, ratings and badges are stored in your browser only. Online games send your name and your moves to your opponent's browser and nothing else, and there is no account to create. Source code is on GitHub under the MIT licence. If you find a rules mistake, open an issue.
