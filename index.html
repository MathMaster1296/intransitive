    How to play

    Intransitive was designed by webgoatguy. The whole rulebook fits on this page, and a first game takes about ten minutes.

    The board

    The board is nine squares by nine. Blue's home corner is a1 at the bottom left and red's is i9 at the top right. Each side starts with ten pieces: three rocks, four papers and three scissors, arranged in three diagonal rows with the scissors closest to the middle. Blue moves first.

    Moving

    On your turn you must move exactly one of your pieces one square in any direction: sideways, up and down, or diagonally. Every piece moves the same way. Passing is not allowed.

    Capturing

    Rock captures scissors, scissors captures paper, and paper captures rock. To capture, move your piece onto an enemy piece it beats, and the enemy piece is removed. You can never move onto one of your own pieces, and you can never move onto an enemy piece your piece does not beat, which includes an enemy piece of the same type. You cannot move there at all.

    There are no ties and no bounces. A rock next to a rock just sits there.

    Winning

    The first player to move any piece into the opponent's home corner wins. Blue is aiming for i9 and red is aiming for a1. It does not matter which piece gets there, and capturing into the corner counts.

    Because a piece can only be captured by the type that beats it, a piece sitting in its own corner is a wall against two of the three enemy types. That is the basis of passive defence, which comes up a lot in the strategy notes.

    Two rules you will rarely need

    There is no stalemate. If it is your turn and you have no legal move, you lose immediately. In practice this almost only happens when you have no pieces left.

    There is a stagnation rule. If 100 moves go by with no capture at all, the game is a draw. There is no draw by repetition. Most games are decided well before move 50, so this rule mostly matters against very defensive opponents.

    Notation

    Moves are written as the starting square, a dash or an x for a capture, and the destination, so c5-d6 is a quiet move and d6xd7 is a capture. Moves are numbered in pairs the way chess does it. The move list on the play page uses this format, and the copy and load buttons let you move games between this site and the official one.

    Strategy notes

    This page collects the ideas from webgoatguy's How To Play & Basic Strategy video, with diagrams you can check against the board. Watch the video for the worked games. This page is the reference card.

    Three kinds of advantage

    Material is the simple one: you have more pieces than the other side. It is exactly as good as it sounds.

    Position comes in two sizes. Macro position means your pieces are closer to the enemy corner than theirs are to yours. A cramped opponent has fewer useful moves, and more importantly the endgame is a race, so trading down into an endgame favours whoever is already further forward. Micro position is about where pieces stand relative to each other: an isolated enemy scissors with your rock nearby is a target, and a fork is a piece attacking two things at once.

    Matchup is the one that is unique to this game. Each of your piece types is only worth something if the enemy has pieces it can capture, and each enemy type is only dangerous if you have pieces it can capture. A side with a single scissors against several enemy papers is in trouble, because one scissors can only answer one paper at a time. Two papers attacking two different places will break through. In general a balanced distribution is the safest, but the right distribution depends on what the other side has left, and it is worth counting the pieces on both sides every few moves.

    Whoever holds two of the three advantages usually wins.

    Rings

    Draw the squares that are one move from a corner, then two, then three. Each of those L-shaped bands is a ring, and every square on a ring is the same number of moves from the corner. Because pieces move like kings, the ring number is the only distance that matters. The rings option on the play page paints them on the board for either corner.

    Passive and active defence

    Sooner or later an enemy piece slips past your pieces and starts running for your corner. You have two ways to stop it.

    A passive defence puts a piece in your own corner that the runner cannot capture, which means a piece of the same type or the type that beats it. The runner is still alive and still a threat, but it cannot finish. The rule is simple: you have to reach the corner first, so you have to stay ahead of the runner in rings. If the runner is on ring 5 and it is your move, your blocker needs to step onto ring 4 now, then ring 3 when the runner reaches ring 4, and so on. Do that and you will always arrive first, unless one of your own pieces is standing in the way.

    An active defence uses the piece type that beats the runner and captures it. The same ring rule mostly applies, with one bit of extra slack: a defender that is one ring behind the runner can still catch it if both are on the same side of the ring, because it can shadow the runner along the edge. From the other side of the ring it never catches up.

    Trades

    Each side starts with four papers but only three rocks and three scissors, so losing a paper costs less than losing a rock or a scissors. A trade that leaves you with three of each and your opponent with a hole in their distribution is a good trade even at equal material. A trade that leaves you with one scissors is usually a bad one, unless you get real position out of it. When an opponent offers a trade, ask what the counts look like afterwards before you accept.

    The extreme case is a sacrifice for matchup: if you have no scissors at all, every enemy paper is immortal, and a lone immortal paper can beat three pieces in the endgame.

    Traps and tempo

    A piece with no safe squares is as good as captured, and you do not have to collect it right away. Two moves is a lot in this game, since two moves is often enough to win outright, so leave a trapped piece where it is and spend the moves on an attack instead. The same logic runs the other way: when your pieces are scattered and several are isolated, retreat and regroup before your opponent starts collecting them.

    Endgames

    Once the board thins out, macro position and matchup decide almost everything, and the variety is enormous. One rock, one paper and one scissors against two rocks and one paper plays nothing like two papers against a rock and a scissors. A common pattern for the stronger side is to park one piece in the home corner as a permanent block and walk the others forward. webgoatguy has played around fifty games and says no two endgames have looked alike.

    About this site

    The site is plain HTML, CSS and JavaScript with no build step, and it runs entirely in your browser. The rules engine follows the official implementation exactly, including the no-stalemate rule and the draw after 100 moves without a capture, and it uses the same move notation, so games can be copied between the two sites.

    The computer opponent is an alpha-beta search that runs in a background thread. Its evaluation is a direct translation of the video's advice: it counts material, penalises lopsided distributions, rewards pieces that are closer to the enemy corner, and checks whether any runner can still be stopped using the ring rule. Easy looks two moves ahead and makes deliberate mistakes, medium looks four moves ahead, and hard searches as deep as it can in about two seconds. The puzzle solutions were checked with the same search.
